Edinburgh — "Auld Reekie"

Edinburgh Castle sits on an extinct volcano. The volcanic rock is about 350 million years old. Scotland has over 2,000 castles scattered across the country.
Scotland's national animal is the unicorn. It's been a symbol of Scottish heraldry for centuries, representing purity and power. Look for it on royal coats of arms throughout Edinburgh.
There's a knighted penguin at Edinburgh Zoo. Sir Nils Olav is a king penguin who holds the rank of Major General in the Norwegian King's Guard. He has his own medal collection and inspects troops during official visits.
A hidden underground city lies beneath Edinburgh. Mary King's Close is a 17th-century street that was sealed up and built over. It's now a tourist attraction where you can walk through the eerily preserved underground rooms.
The Picts were here first. Long before Edinburgh was Edinburgh, it was part of the kingdom of the Picts — a fierce, mysterious people who dominated Scotland from around 300–900 AD. They're famous for their intricate carved stones covered in symbols that scholars still can't fully decode. The name "Edinburgh" may come from "Din Eidyn," a Pictish/Brittonic fortress. You'll see Pictish stone carvings in the National Museum of Scotland.
The Black Dinner of 1440 inspired Game of Thrones. At Edinburgh Castle, the young Earl of Douglas and his brother were invited to dine with the 10-year-old King James II. A black bull's head was placed on the table as a symbol of death, and the brothers were dragged outside and beheaded. This event directly inspired the Red Wedding scene in Game of Thrones. George R.R. Martin has confirmed the connection.
Edinburgh has more listed buildings than anywhere in the world. The Old Town and New Town together form a UNESCO World Heritage Site, with over 4,500 listed buildings.
The Royal Mile isn't actually a mile. It's 1.12 miles (1.81 km) from Edinburgh Castle to the Palace of Holyroodhouse.
The Old Town's layout looks like a fish skeleton. The Royal Mile is the spine and the narrow closes/wynds are the ribs. "Closes" were private and literally locked at night; "wynds" were public thoroughfares. This distinction was a medieval security system built into the urban design.
The South Bridge Vaults went from business hub to criminal underworld. When South Bridge opened in 1788, the arches beneath attracted legitimate businesses. Flooding drove them out, and the vaults became a sordid world of brothels, gambling, and unlicensed taverns before being sealed in the mid-19th century and forgotten until rediscovered in the 1980s.
The Georgian New Town was designed by a 23-year-old nobody. In 1766, unknown architect James Craig won the competition for Edinburgh's New Town with a revolutionary grid plan. He beat out established architects. Many wealthy New Town residents grew rich through involvement in the slave trade — the elegant Georgian architecture hides a troubling financial foundation.
🔍 Royal Mile
Heart of Midlothian — A heart-shaped mosaic of red and black cobblestones embedded in the pavement near St Giles' Cathedral. It marks where the Old Tolbooth prison once stood. Locals traditionally spit on it as a sign of contempt for the old prison. Look down or you'll walk right over it.
Paisley Close — Look for a carved face of a boy above the close entrance with the inscription "Heave away chaps, I'm no dead yet." 12-year-old Joseph McIvor was pulled alive from a tenement collapse in 1861.
Dunbar's Close Garden — A hidden 17th-century formal garden tucked behind an unassuming gate on Canongate (bottom of the Royal Mile). Within 50 feet of the crowded Mile, most tourists walk right past it.
Witches' Memorial Fountain — A cast iron fountain near the top of the Royal Mile commemorating 300+ women executed for witchcraft between 1479–1722. More women were killed here than anywhere else in Scotland.
Camera Obscura — At the top of the Royal Mile near Edinburgh Castle. Over 100 optical illusions across 5 floors, including the original 17th-century Camera Obscura device that projects live views of Edinburgh's streets onto a screen using mirrors. Great for teens.
Mary King's Close — An entire buried 17th-century street network underground beneath the Royal Mile, abandoned after plague. Requires a pre-booked guided tour. You walk through actual 17th-century alleyways below modern street level.
Anchor Close — Where the first editions of the Encyclopaedia Britannica and Robert Burns' poems were printed (1768 and 1787). A tiny plaque marks the spot but almost nobody notices it.
🔍 St Giles' Cathedral
Thistle Chapel — An ornate chapel with enormous carved wooden seats, each topped with wildly decorative crests. The craftsmanship is over-the-top in the best way. Look up at the carved ceiling — three angels playing bagpipes are hidden in the woodwork. Two blend almost invisibly into the beams near the door, the third is carved from stone overlooking a window. They're the only Scottish instruments in the cathedral's celestial imagery.
Jenny Geddes' Stool Riot — When King Charles I tried to impose a new prayer book at St Giles' in 1637, a local woman named Jenny Geddes picked up her prayer stool and threw it at the preacher, sparking a riot. One woman's frustration became a turning point in Scottish religious history.
John Knox's Grave — The famous Protestant reformer is buried under parking space #23 in Parliament Square, right behind the cathedral. A small brass plate in the pavement is the only marker. You're literally standing on his grave.
🔍 Edinburgh Castle
Mons Meg — A massive 15th-century siege cannon (over 6 tons) sitting on the castle battlements. When fired at the wedding of James IV in 1558, the cannonball was reportedly found 2 miles away in Wardie.
The Stone of Destiny — Returned to Scotland in 1996 after 700 years in England (stolen by Edward I in 1296). It sits in the Crown Room. Scottish kings were crowned on this stone for centuries.
The One O'Clock Gun — Fired every day at 1pm (except Sundays). Originally helped ships in the Firth of Forth set their maritime clocks. Still fires daily — you'll hear it across the city. Stand on the castle esplanade at 12:59 for the full experience.
David's Tower — Rediscovered in a Coal Cellar — In 1912, a hidden tower built in the 1380s by King David II was rediscovered behind a canteen coal cellar window. It originally stood 100 feet high and had been forgotten for centuries.
A 1715 Jacobite Siege Failed Because the Ladder Was Too Short — The Jacobites brought a siege ladder that turned out to be shorter than the castle ramparts. One of history's more embarrassing military failures, caused by a measurement error.
Caribbean Pirates Were Held Here — The castle reportedly held 21 pirates from Caribbean raids, who were ultimately sentenced to death. Few realize Edinburgh Castle served as a prison for international criminals from across the ocean.
🔍 Greyfriars Kirkyard
Tom Riddle's Grave — A headstone for "Thomas Riddell" (died 1806), the real person whose name inspired Voldemort. The graveyard is full of Harry Potter name inspirations: look for Moodie (Mad-Eye Moody), McGonagall, Scrymgeour, and even Potter headstones scattered throughout.
Greyfriars Bobby — A statue of a Skye Terrier who reportedly guarded his owner's grave for 14 years (1858–1872) sits outside the kirkyard gates. His nose is shiny gold from everyone rubbing it for luck.
The Covenanters' Prison — A locked section of the kirkyard where 1,200 Covenanters were imprisoned in 1679. Said to be one of the most haunted spots in Edinburgh, home to the "Mackenzie Poltergeist."
🔍 Palace of Holyroodhouse
David Rizzio's 57 Dagger Wounds — Mary Queen of Scots' Italian secretary David Rizzio was murdered in her private chambers on March 9, 1566, sustaining 57 dagger wounds. He was stabbed while hiding behind the heavily pregnant Mary. The murder was orchestrated partly because Rizzio's enemies convinced Mary's jealous husband Darnley that Rizzio had fathered her child — probably false, but deadly enough.
The Bloodstain Room — You can still visit the exact room where Rizzio was murdered. Red ink is deliberately placed on the floor to show visitors where the bloodstains supposedly remain. The palace maintains this theatrical touch as part of the tourist experience.
🔍 Sir Walter Scott Monument
The Designer Used a Fake Name and Died Before Completion — George Meikle Kemp, a 45-year-old joiner and self-taught architect, submitted his winning design under the pseudonym "John Morvo" because he feared his lack of formal credentials would disqualify him. He drowned in the Union Canal before the monument was completed. One of Scotland's most iconic monuments was designed by someone working under a fake name to overcome class barriers.
23 Stonemasons Died Building It — The oily Binny sandstone required intense carving, making the masons vulnerable to silicosis. As many as 23 of the 70 stonemasons died from occupational disease during or shortly after construction. The beautiful monument celebrates literature but represents a largely forgotten health crisis.
🔍 Edinburgh Ghost Tours
South Bridge Vaults — The most common ghost tour route. The vaults were sealed in the mid-19th century and forgotten until rediscovered in the 1980s. The most famous haunting is "The Watcher" or "Mr. Boots" — witnesses consistently describe the same heavy footsteps with a distinctive scuff or shuffle as they come to a standstill.
The Bagpiper's Tunnel Mystery — Legend says a young boy was sent to play bagpipes through tunnels beneath Edinburgh Castle. The music stopped abruptly when he reached Tron Kirk, and he was never seen again — suggesting the tunnel system has unknown depths still uncharted.
Greyfriars Covenanters' Prison — 1,200 Covenanters were imprisoned in a locked section of Greyfriars Kirkyard in 1679. Home to the "Mackenzie Poltergeist," said to be one of the most documented poltergeist cases in the world.
🔍 Other Edinburgh
The Elephant House Café — Despite calling itself "The Birthplace of Harry Potter," J.K. Rowling didn't visit until years after she'd started writing the series. She conceived Harry Potter on a delayed train in 1990 and wrote the first chapters in Portugal. The café's toilets were famously covered in HP fan graffiti — staff repainted daily but fans worked faster. After a devastating fire in 2021, the café was closed for 1,587 days before reopening.
National Museum of Scotland Roof Terrace — A secret terrace on the top floor of the museum on Chambers Street with panoramic views of Edinburgh Castle. Most visitors don't know it exists. Free entry.
Sir Walter Scott Monument — The second-largest monument to a writer anywhere in the world (only José Martí's in Havana is taller). 287 steps to the top for panoramic views. 64 statuettes of characters from his novels decorate the exterior. Arthur Conan Doyle and Sir Walter Scott both lived in the Georgian New Town.

Edinburgh Ghost Tour

Edinburgh has a genuinely creepy history — witch burnings, plague, underground vaults. The ghost tours are atmospheric and fun, not campy. Great for a group! Listen for the story of "Gardy Loo!" — the warning cry before chamber pots were emptied out windows.

The Elephant House Café

The Edinburgh café where J.K. Rowling wrote parts of Harry Potter. Grab a coffee, enjoy the view of Edinburgh Castle, and channel your inner wizard.

Stirling Castle, the Trossachs

Stirling Castle & the Trossachs

Someone once tried to fly from Stirling Castle. In the 1500s, an Italian alchemist named John Damian strapped on feathered wings and leapt from the castle battlements. He landed in a dunghill and broke his thighbone. He blamed the chicken feathers — he should have used eagle feathers, apparently.
The world's oldest football was found at Stirling Castle — discovered hidden behind wall panelling in the Queen's chamber. Mary, Queen of Scots was reportedly a big sports fan.
This is Braveheart country. The Battle of Stirling Bridge (1297) where William Wallace defeated the English took place just below the castle. The Wallace Monument towers over the landscape nearby. The castle has passed between Scottish and English control more times than anyone can count.
Stirling Castle has been besieged at least 16 times. More than any other castle in Scotland. Whoever held Stirling controlled the route between the Highlands and Lowlands.
The Battle of Stirling Bridge (1297). William Wallace's famous victory happened just below the castle. The English army tried to cross a narrow bridge and Wallace waited until half were across, then attacked. The bridge collapsed under the weight of armored soldiers.
A single poem invented tourism in the Trossachs. Sir Walter Scott's 1810 poem "The Lady of the Lake" was a blockbuster (25,000 copies sold in 8 months). It immediately made the Trossachs a literary pilgrimage, drawing Keats, Coleridge, and later Jules Verne. Modern tourism marketing 200 years early.
🔍 Stirling Castle
The Stirling Heads — 38 surviving metre-wide 16th-century carved oak portrait medallions in the ceiling of the King's Inner Hall. Kings, queens, mythological figures — each one different, created around 1540. Scotland's great art treasures hiding in plain sight above your head.
Royal Graffiti — Behind a locked door in the Princes Tower, there's historic graffiti reading "James 6" — possibly an autograph by young King James VI himself.
Roof Unicorns & Lions — Look up at the Great Hall roof ridge for carved stone unicorns and lions perching on top. Easy to miss if you don't look up.
Seven "Hunt of the Unicorn" Tapestries — Hang in the Queen's Inner Hall, based on the 1500s tapestry series. The castle is essentially a unicorn museum.
🔍 The Trossachs
Rob Roy Born by Loch Katrine — The outlaw Rob Roy MacGregor was born in 1671 at Glengyle on Loch Katrine's banks and buried at nearby Balquhidder. Scott's 1817 novel romanticized his legend, but the real story is nearly as dramatic.
Queen Victoria's Royal Cottage She Never Visited — Victoria arrived in 1859 and was rowed on Loch Katrine. An expensive Royal Cottage was built overlooking the loch for her stay. But the 21-gun salute welcoming her shattered the windows. She never stayed. The cottage became staff accommodation for Scottish Water.
White Milestone Markers — Markers installed for Victoria's 1859 journey still line the roadsides, creating a 150+ year-old "royal route" you can trace.

Glencoe, Loch Ness & Culloden

Loch Ness is 24 miles long and up to 700 feet deep. Stories of a mysterious creature living in its dark waters have persisted for over 1,500 years. Recent eDNA testing found evidence of giant eels, but no definitive "Nessie." Keep your camera ready on the boat cruise.
Loch Ness contains more fresh water than all the lakes in England and Wales combined — it's 230m deep and 37km long. If Nessie is in there, she's got plenty of room to hide.
The Massacre of Glencoe (1692) — Government soldiers were billeted with the MacDonald clan for 12 days under sacred Highland hospitality before rising at 5am and murdering 38 men, women, and children. The betrayal of hospitality — not the killing itself — is what made it infamous. Still a raw subject.

Loch Ness Boat Cruise

Keep your eyes peeled and your camera ready. The water is dark with peat — you can barely see a few feet below the surface. Will you be the one to finally spot Nessie?

Highland midges alert: Tiny biting insects that swarm in the Scottish Highlands in summer. They're harmless but annoying. Bug repellent with DEET or Smidge brand (sold locally) works best. They're worst at dawn and dusk.
🔍 Glencoe
Glencoe Lochan (A Little Piece of Canada) — Woodland planted in 1895 by Lord Strathcona, a Scottish-born fur trader who made his fortune in Canada. He created this landscape to ease his Canadian wife's homesickness. A pocket of the Canadian Rockies in the Highlands.
Monty Python's Bridge of Death — The "Bridge of Death" scene from Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975) was filmed at the Meeting of Three Waters in Glencoe, where waterfalls converge into the River Coe. About a 15-minute walk from the A82. "Answer me these questions three..."
Hidden Valley (Coire Gabhail) — A dramatic high-level glen historically used by the MacDonald clan to hide stolen cattle. The steep entrance conceals the width of the valley — you can't see it until you're inside.
James Bond's Skyfall — The road through Glencoe was used for the driving scenes in Skyfall (2012). The fictional "Skyfall Lodge" was set here (though the actual building was constructed on a set at Hankley Common in England).
Glencoe is a 420-million-year-old supervolcano — The dramatic peaks are the skeletal remains of an ancient supervolcano from the Silurian period. It's one of the world's best examples of a subsidence caldera — the phenomenon was first scientifically described here.
The true Glencoe death toll was double — While 38 MacDonalds were killed by troops, up to 76 more people died from exposure fleeing into the winter mountains. The massacre's real horror was starvation and freezing.
A coin hoard from the massacre was found in 2023 — Archaeologists discovered coins hidden in a house linked to MacIain (the clan chief). They believe someone stashed them while fleeing. We're still finding physical evidence 330+ years later.
🔍 Loch Ness
The First Nessie Sighting — St. Columba allegedly encountered a "water beast" in the River Ness in 565 AD, making the legend nearly 1,500 years old. The modern craze started in 1933 when a couple reported seeing "an enormous animal" crossing the road.
Urquhart Castle — The most-photographed Nessie-spotting location. The castle was deliberately blown up in 1692 to prevent Jacobites from using it. What you're seeing is the result of intentional destruction, not natural decay.
Steve Feltham — A man who gave up his home and girlfriend in 1991 to hunt for Nessie full-time. He still lives in a converted mobile library on the shore of Loch Ness. He holds the Guinness World Record for the longest continuous vigil for Nessie.
Loch Ness sits on the Great Glen Fault — A 62-mile geological fault line that cuts the Highlands in half. The loch wasn't formed by glaciers like most Scottish lochs — it's the visible scar of a massive continental fracture. The fault creates underwater fissures and cave systems still being explored.
Seismic activity may explain "Nessie" sightings — Italian geologist Luigi Piccardi documented a correlation between Nessie sightings and periods of seismic activity along the fault. Tremors may cause underwater disturbances that generate unusual wave patterns. The "monster" may be the earth itself.
🔍 Culloden
The Battle Lasted Only 40 Minutes — On April 16, 1746, the final Jacobite Rising ended in under an hour. The aftermath was far longer and far worse — "Butcher" Cumberland's forces killed wounded and prisoners for days afterward.
Clan Grave Markers — Simple stones mark where each clan's dead were buried in mass graves. The Cameron stone, Fraser stone, and others still stand. You're walking on a battlefield graveyard.
Archaeology is rewriting the battle — Since 2001, excavations have found musket balls, bayonets, buttons, and a pewter cross (possibly a good-luck charm). These finds suggest the brutal hand-to-hand combat occurred much further south than historians thought. We're still rewriting Culloden's geography 275+ years later.
The battlefield was nearly erased — After 1746, farmland encroached on the site. By 1835, a road was built through the middle. Spruce plantations were planted, completely obscuring the original moorland. The site became invisible as a battlefield until 21st-century archaeology "rediscovered" it.

Loch Lomond, Glasgow city walk

Loch Lomond & Glasgow

Loch Lomond is the largest expanse of fresh water in Britain — five miles wide at its broadest point with over 30 islands, three of which are inhabited. It's a favorite recreational spot for Scots.
Glasgow is called "The Dear Green Place" because of its many parks. It's the birthplace of Scottish Opera, Scottish Ballet, and the Royal Scottish National Orchestra. In Glasgow, a child is called a wean (rhymes with "rain") — short for "wee ane" (little one).
Glasgow's motto is "Let Glasgow Flourish" — The city's coat of arms features a bird, a tree, a bell, and a fish with a ring — all from miracles attributed to St Mungo, the city's patron saint. Locals remember them with the rhyme: "The bird that never flew, the tree that never grew, the bell that never rang, the fish that never swam."
Glasgow was once the "Second City of the British Empire" — In the Victorian era, Glasgow produced more ships, trains, and heavy machinery than almost anywhere on Earth. The industrial heritage is still visible in the architecture.
George Square was built on a horse slaughterhouse — Around 1772, the land was Ramshorn Croft, a filthy swamp used for slaughtering horses. After a decade of work, it became one of Britain's grandest Georgian squares.
George Square was supposed to have a King George III statue — After America declared independence in 1776 and Glasgow lost its tobacco trade, the statue was quietly canceled. A political embarrassment erased from the city's memory.
Glasgow City Chambers has more marble than the Vatican — The three-story Carrara marble staircase is the largest in Western Europe. 1.5 million individual mosaic tiles were laid by hand. The lucky marble lion inside has a worn-smooth nose from decades of council workers rubbing it for luck.
🔍 Glasgow City Walk Sites
The Duke of Wellington's Traffic Cone — An equestrian statue in Royal Exchange Square with a traffic cone permanently on the Duke's head. Started in the 1980s (probably by drunk revelers), locals replace the cone every time the council removes it. Named one of Lonely Planet's "Top 10 Most Bizarre Monuments on Earth." Symbol of Glasgow's irreverent spirit.
Lobey Dosser Statue — The world's only two-legged horse statue, on Woodlands Road in the West End. Depicts Sheriff Lobey Dosser riding his magical two-legged horse "El Fideldo" from a beloved Glasgow comic strip (1949–1956).
Homeless Jesus — A life-sized bronze sculpture of a shrouded figure sleeping on a bench, only the crucifixion wounds on the feet visible. Tucked around the back of St George's Tron Church on Nelson Mandela Place (off Buchanan Street). Easy to miss and very powerful.
Finnich Glen / Devil's Pulpit — Near Loch Lomond (30 min south of Glasgow). A deep sandstone gorge with moss-covered walls and water that appears blood-red from the sandstone bed. Otherworldly. Accessed via the "Devil's Steps" (treacherous after rain — be careful).
Strathclyde University — Founded in 1796 as the "Andersonian Institute" with the radical mission of "Useful Learning" — practical subjects rather than pure theory. Became Britain's first technological university in 1964. The 1967 Architecture Building (designed by its own Professor of Architecture) is a Grade B listed Brutalist masterpiece with one of the UK's largest wall murals hidden inside.
Robert Burns' Statue in George Square — Funded by public subscription from 40,000 working people who each contributed a shilling. An estimated 30,000+ gathered on Burns' birthday in 1877 for the unveiling. A monument funded by ordinary workers, not wealthy patrons.
Glasgow's Scott Monument Predates Edinburgh's — The Sir Walter Scott column in George Square was the first public monument to Scott, built almost a decade before Edinburgh's more famous (and larger) one.
🔍 Loch Lomond
22+ Islands and Britain's Largest Freshwater Island — Loch Lomond contains over 22 islands and 27 islets. Inchmurrin is the largest freshwater island in the British Isles.
Robert the Bruce's War Yew Trees — In the 14th century, King Robert the Bruce planted ancient yew trees on Inchlonaig Island specifically to supply bows for his archers at the Battle of Bannockburn. The island is literally called "The Island of Yews."
Australian Wallabies in Scotland — Inchconnachan island is home to a colony of red-necked wallabies — Australian marsupials living wild in Loch Lomond. Decades after their mysterious arrival, nobody can agree on how they got there.
Vikings Hauled Longboats Over Land — Viking raiders sailed up Loch Long, hauled their longboats overland at the narrow neck near Tarbet, and sacked islands in Loch Lomond. A Norse raid route deep into the Highlands.
"The Bonnie Banks" May Be a Prisoner's Farewell — The song likely originated as a 1745 Jacobite prisoner's last poem, possibly after the retreat following Culloden. The "low road" refers to the soul's passage through the underworld — the singer knows he's going to die.

Giant's Causeway

The Giant's Causeway has about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns formed by a volcanic eruption 50–60 million years ago. Most are hexagonal, but some have 4, 5, 7, or 8 sides. Legend says the giant Finn McCool built it as a walkway to Scotland to fight a rival giant — but when he saw how big the Scottish giant was, his wife disguised him as a baby. The Scottish giant, terrified of how big the "baby" was, fled back across the sea, destroying the causeway behind him.
60% of the columns are mathematically perfect hexagons. Not accidental. It's the most energy-efficient way for cooling basalt to crack and contract. Nature optimizing for minimum energy expenditure.
The Causeway extends underwater. What you see is just the tip. The lowest columnar lava flow thickens to over 70 metres beneath the water, compared to 20 metres on the headlands. A vast underwater extension remains mostly unexplored.
Led Zeppelin's "Houses of the Holy" album cover featured the Causeway. The 1973 cover featured the Causeway with eerie child figures. But the site has appeared in art for centuries and was named one of only 100 global "geological heritage sites" in 2022.
Scientists only recently cracked the formation temperature. The exact cooling temperature (between 1,544–1,634°F) was only reproduced in the lab recently. A site 60 million years old is still yielding scientific breakthroughs.
Currency switch: You're now in Northern Ireland, which is part of the UK. You're still using British Pounds (£) here, same as Scotland. The switch to Euros happens when you cross into the Republic of Ireland (Dublin).
🔍 Easter Eggs & Hidden Gems
The Camel & the Giant's Boot — Distinctive rock formations among the basalt columns that look exactly like a camel and a giant's boot. A natural scavenger hunt through the formations.
The Scotland Connection — You're standing at one end of a geologically connected basalt formation. The other end is Fingal's Cave on the Isle of Staffa in Scotland, 150km away across the sea. The "causeway" myth isn't entirely wrong — the same volcanic eruption created both.
Four Walking Trails — Most tourists only see the main columns. Four marked trails offer emptier perspectives and formations most visitors never reach.

Belfast sightseeing, Titanic Quarter, travel to Dublin

Belfast & Arrival in Dublin

The Titanic was built in Belfast at Harland & Wolff shipyard. The Titanic, Olympic, and Britannic were all built here. The famous yellow cranes "Samson" and "Goliath" still dominate the skyline. Belfast's dark joke: "She was fine when she left here." The Titanic Belfast museum now stands on the exact site where the ship was constructed. Its angular aluminium exterior is designed to look like a ship's hull, with a height (38.5m) exactly matching the Titanic's hull height.
Belfast has the oldest pub in Ireland. McHugh's Bar (1711) in Queen's Square. Though several pubs across Ireland dispute this claim.
Dublin's Ha'Penny Bridge gets its name from the ha'penny (half-penny) toll that pedestrians once had to pay to cross it from 1816 to 1919. Before the bridge, people crossed the Liffey by ferry — the bridge was built by the ferry operator as a replacement. Citizens had a one-year trial to reject it if they found it "objectionable." During the 1916 Easter Rising, rebels crossed without paying. The 2014 restoration used 85% original metalwork.
You're now in the Republic of Ireland! Switch to Euros (€). Cards work almost everywhere, but it's good to have some cash. And remember: tipping is 10–15% in restaurants if there's no service charge already on the bill.
🔍 Easter Eggs & Hidden Gems
Titanic Quarter
"Big Fish" Salmon Sculpture — A modern art installation along the Lagan walk. Look closely at the scales — they contain printed images showing Belfast's entire history, reading like a visual comic book of the city.
The Titanic Slipways — The actual slipways where the Titanic and Olympic were built are marked on the ground outside the museum. You're standing where the ship was born.
Peace Walls & Murals
Falls Road Murals Include Palestinian and Basque Symbols — The Nationalist/Republican murals don't just depict Irish history — they include symbols of Palestinian liberation and Basque freedom, showing how Belfast's struggle connected to global revolutionary movements.
The A-Z History Mural Is a Coded Timeline — One Shankill Road mural arranges the street's entire history as an alphabetical grid. Each letter corresponds to a specific event, location, or figure. It's a coded visual encyclopedia requiring intimate local knowledge to decode.
The Lower Shankill Women's Quilt Mural — In an area known for paramilitary imagery, artist Lesley Cherry helped local women create murals designed like family quilts. Each woman contributed her family stories of care, loss, and love — a deliberately gentle counternarrative.
Titanic to Healing Murals — Entire sections of formerly political walls in East Belfast now feature Titanic and shipyard imagery instead. The shift from conflict to maritime heritage is visible proof of community healing, literally written on the walls.
The Gates Still Close — Some Peace Wall gates between Catholic and Protestant neighborhoods are still locked at night. This isn't historical — it's ongoing. The walls were supposed to come down by 2023 but the deadline has been quietly extended.
Belfast City Hall
Hidden Marble Creatures — Look closely at the marble pillars and banisters inside City Hall. Craftsmen carved tiny hidden figures into the stonework: mice, angels, and other creatures tucked into corners and behind columns. A marble scavenger hunt.

Jul 15 — Dublin
Trinity College, Book of Kells, Phoenix Park, Aviva Stadium, pub dinner

Dublin Landmarks

Dublin means "Black Pool". From the Irish Dubh Linn (dark pool), referring to a tidal pool where the River Poddle met the Liffey. The Irish name for Dublin is Baile Átha Cliath (Town of the Hurdled Ford), which is completely different and refers to a different feature.
Dublin has the youngest population of any European capital. About 50% of the population is under 25.
The Book of Kells was handwritten by Irish monks around 800 AD using multicolored ink made from plants and crushed insects. The intricate Celtic designs are so detailed that some can only be fully appreciated with a magnifying glass. It's housed in the Long Room at Trinity College — a stunning 65-meter library with over 200,000 old books.
Phoenix Park in Dublin is 1,752 acres — the largest enclosed park in any European capital. It has free-roaming wild deer, cricket pitches, and the President of Ireland's residence. The name has nothing to do with the mythical bird — it comes from the Irish "fionn uisce" meaning "clear water."
St. Valentine's actual remains are in Dublin at Whitefriar Street Church, donated in 1836 by Pope Gregory XVI.
Dublin's oldest pub, The Brazen Head, opened in 1198. That's over 800 years of pouring pints.
🔍 Easter Eggs & Hidden Gems
Trinity College & Book of Kells
The Long Room & Star Wars — Trinity's Old Library Long Room (65m of barrel-vaulted ceiling, 200,000 ancient books) was the visual inspiration for the Jedi Archives in Star Wars: Attack of the Clones. Lucasfilm initially denied this but the resemblance is undeniable. Compare photos side by side.
Hidden Microscopic Portraits in the Book of Kells — A 20th-century scholar used photography to enlarge one decorative panel and discovered a tiny human face buried within the knotwork — invisible to the naked eye. Medieval monks were creating hidden micro-art that wouldn't be discovered for 700+ years.
Tiny Animals Hidden in the Illuminations — Within the spirals and margins are mice, cats, otters, and other creatures, many no larger than a fingernail. Scholars believe monks snuck these in as jokes or personal signatures. Medieval monks invented Easter eggs in artwork 12 centuries before digital media.
The Book Was Stolen in 1007 and Thrown in a Ditch — The medieval Annals of Ulster describe it as "the most precious object in the western world." It was found after two months buried under sod. The missing pages at the beginning and end were likely destroyed when the binding was ripped off.
The Book Was Later Used for Real Estate Records — In the 11th–12th centuries, blank pages were repurposed to record property transactions. One of history's greatest artworks became a medieval ledger.
Museum Building Carvings — The exterior (built 1853–1857) is covered in intricate stone carvings of animals and plants from when it housed the Geology department. Hunt for your favorite hidden animal.
The Campanile Superstition — Trinity students believe that if you walk under the Campanile (bell tower) when the bell is ringing, you'll fail your exams. Watch students scatter when it chimes.
Ha'Penny Bridge
The Toll — Named for the half-penny toll charged to cross from 1816 to 1919. Before the bridge, people crossed the Liffey by ferry — the bridge was built by the ferry operator as a replacement.
Citizens Had a One-Year Trial to Reject It — A clause stated that if Dublin citizens found the bridge "objectionable" during the first year, it would be removed at no cost. The first ten days of crossing were toll-free as an incentive.
Easter Rising Rebels Crossed Without Paying — During the 1916 Rising, Hibernian Rifles member J.J. Scollan proudly noted he and fellow rebels crossed the bridge without paying: "the toll man was still on duty but did not get it."
The 2014 Restoration Used 85% Original Metalwork — Engineers considered 52 different design options. They removed over 1,000 individual rail pieces for repair. The 85% original requirement was to maintain UNESCO heritage status.
Molly Malone Statue
"The Tart with the Cart" — Dublin's irreverent nickname for the statue of the famous fishmonger. Her bronze décolletage is noticeably shinier than the rest of the statue from people rubbing it for luck. (Same energy as Greyfriars Bobby's nose.)
She Probably Never Existed — Despite the famous song, there's no historical evidence that Molly Malone was a real person. The statue's "birthday" (June 13) was invented for a 1988 Dublin millennium celebration.
St Patrick's Cathedral
Jonathan Swift's Self-Written Epitaph — The author of Gulliver's Travels was Dean of St Patrick's for 32 years (1713–1745) and wrote his own epitaph, which translates to: "He has gone where fierce indignation can no longer lacerate his heart." W.B. Yeats called it the greatest epitaph in history.
The Door of Reconciliation — In 1492, two feuding Irish earls (Ormonde and Kildare) fought inside the cathedral. Kildare cut a hole in the Chapter House door and stuck his arm through as a peace gesture, risking having it cut off. This is allegedly the origin of the phrase "chancing your arm."
The Spire (O'Connell Street)
Dublin's Nicknames for the Spire — The 120m stainless steel monument has collected more nicknames than any structure in Ireland: "The Stiletto in the Ghetto," "The Pin in the Bin," "The Stiffy by the Liffey," "The Nail in the Pale," and "The Erection at the Intersection." Dubliners are relentless.
O'Connell Street & GPO
Bullet Holes Deliberately Preserved — The GPO's facade is still pockmarked with bullet holes and shrapnel from the 1916 Easter Rising. Dublin chose to keep them as physical reminders. Most countries would rebuild and erase — Dublin kept the scars.
The Proclamation Was Read During an Active Seizure — Patrick Pearse read the Proclamation of the Irish Republic outside the GPO on April 24, 1916 — while 1,200 armed nationalists were simultaneously seizing the building. A live declaration during a military takeover.
The Ionic Columns Saved the Building — While the interior was devastated, the six enormous Ionic columns at the facade were structurally sound enough that the decision was made to rebuild around them rather than demolish entirely. Columns designed for beauty saved the building from destruction.
Parnell Square
Buck Mulligan Was Born Here — Oliver St John Gogarty, the real person Joyce immortalized as "Buck Mulligan" (the character who opens Ulysses), was born on Parnell Square. Writer, surgeon, and senator — friends with both Yeats and Joyce.
The Hugh Lane Gallery Was the World's First Public Gallery of Modern Art — Founded in 1908 by art collector Sir Hugh Lane. Not just important nationally — revolutionary internationally.
W.B. Yeats Wrote Five Poems About the Hugh Lane Gallery — A major poet dedicated multiple poems to an art gallery, treating an institution with the same attention as landscapes or people.
Garden of Remembrance — Occupies the site where the Irish Volunteers were founded in 1913 and where leaders of the 1916 Rising were held overnight before transport to Kilmainham Gaol. A beautiful garden covering a site of both revolutionary founding and tragic imprisonment.
Phoenix Park
The Phoenix Park Murders Used Surgical Knives — On May 6, 1882, Lord Cavendish and Thomas Burke were assassinated by the Irish National Invincibles using 12-inch surgical knives. The group chose surgical knives deliberately — to avoid making noise in the park.
The Papal Cross Was Supposed to Be Temporary — The 116-foot white cross was erected for Pope John Paul II's 1979 visit. Architect Ronnie Tallon mentioned the Office of Public Works planned to remove it. Taoiseach Charlie Haughey vetoed the removal by phone call. It's been there ever since.
The Hole in the Wall Pub — British soldiers from nearby barracks used to get drinks through a literal hole in the wall without officially leaving the park grounds. The pub still exists and the name stuck.
Glasnevin Cemetery
Nearly 800,000 People Lie in Unmarked Mass Graves — Due to the Great Famine and cholera epidemics, hundreds of thousands were buried anonymously. Over a million total burials — more than the current population of Dublin.
Gunfights Between Mourners and Body Snatchers — In the 1830s, gunshots were exchanged between mourners and grave robbers. The locks, gates, and watchtowers were built specifically to prevent body theft.
A Lion Tamer Killed by His Lion — Among the unusual graves: a lion tamer killed by one of the lions he trained.
Famous Graves — Michael Collins, Éamon de Valera, Daniel O'Connell (whose round tower at 51m is the tallest in Ireland), Constance Markievicz, Brendan Behan.
Aviva Stadium
The World's First International Sporting Venue — The original Lansdowne Road, founded in 1872, was the first international sporting venue anywhere. On March 11, 1878, it hosted the first international rugby union test match (Ireland vs. England), making it the world's oldest rugby test venue.
The English Rugby Team Got a Standing Ovation During the Troubles — When threats to visiting teams were real, the English team came to play. When they ran onto the field, they received a spontaneous standing ovation. Captain Bill Pullin said: "We may not be much good but at least we turned up."
134 Years of Continuous Use Before Demolition — Lansdowne Road closed in 2006 and was demolished in 2007 after 134 years. The new Aviva Stadium opened in 2010 on the same site.
Other Dublin
Dublin Statue Nicknames — Dubliners give irreverent nicknames to every public statue. The James Joyce statue is "The Prick with the Stick." The women-on-a-bench statue is "The Hags with the Bags." The former Anna Livia fountain was "The Floozie in the Jacuzzi." Ask locals for more — they love sharing these.
The Father Pat Noise Plaque — A fake commemorative plaque on a Dublin bridge claiming to mark where "Father Pat Noise" drowned in 1919 under suspicious circumstances. It's an elaborate local prank that fooled visitors for years. Peak Dublin humor.

Jul 16 — Galway

Galway & the Arts Festival

The longest place name in Ireland is Muckanaghederdauhaulia in County Galway. It means "pig-marsh between two sea inlets." Good luck pronouncing it.
Galway is the "City of the Tribes" — 14 merchant families (the "Tribes of Galway") controlled the city for centuries. Their family crests appear throughout the city — look for them on buildings, flags, and plaques in Eyre Square.
The Galway International Arts Festival is one of Europe's leading arts festivals, running since 1978. Your visit (mid-July) should overlap with festival dates — expect street performers, installations, and events everywhere.

Galway International Arts Festival

Your visit coincides with the 2026 Galway International Arts Festival (July 13–26)! Expect street performers, music, theatre, and art installations throughout the city. The energy is incredible. 2026 acts include James, The Saw Doctors, Bell X1, and Cian Ducrot.

🔍 Spanish Arch
The 1755 Tsunami Damage — The "Spanish" Arch was partially destroyed by a 10-foot tidal wave from the 1755 Lisbon earthquake. The wave traveled 1,000 miles in just over 4 hours. The damage is still visible on the Caoċ Arch (blind arch). You're looking at earthquake damage from across Europe.
Not Actually Spanish — The arch was built in 1584 to protect merchant quays from looting. It's called "Spanish" because Spanish traders were the main users of the docks. The Galway-Spain trading connection was so strong that the "Spanish Arch" area felt like a little corner of Iberia.
🔍 Eyre Square
The Browne Doorway — An ornate 17th-century doorway from a demolished family mansion, moved to the square in 1905 and now encased in plexiglass as it slowly deteriorates. A ghost of old Galway fading in plain sight.
Tribal Family Flags — Fourteen flag poles in the square fly the crests of Galway's original 14 merchant families. See how many family names you can spot around the city on buildings and plaques.
🔍 St Nicholas' Collegiate Church
The Columbus Connection — Legend says Christopher Columbus prayed here in 1477 before his voyage to the Americas. A bust of Columbus sits inside the church. Whether the story is true is debated, but Galway's maritime links to Spain make it plausible.
The Crusader's Tomb — A 13th-century tomb effigy inside the church depicts a knight in full Crusader armor. One of the oldest such carvings in Ireland.
🔍 Other Galway
Lynch's Window — Built into the wall of St Nicholas' Church on Market Street. In 1493, Mayor James Lynch FitzStephen personally executed his own son Walter for murdering a Spanish merchant because no one else in the city would carry out the sentence. Cited (though debated) as a possible origin of the term "lynch law" — but the real origin is the opposite of mob justice: a father upholding the law.
Nora Barnacle's House — James Joyce's partner (and later wife) Nora Barnacle lived in Galway until 1904. She left for Dublin at age 21 and began her relationship with Joyce. The family home (1894–1940) is now a private museum. The house preserves her pre-fame life.
The Claddagh Ring — Legend says Richard Joyce fashioned the first one — with heart for love, hands for friendship, crown for loyalty. Some versions claim Joyce created it while enslaved as a galley slave. The world's most famous Irish ring may originate from a trauma narrative softened into romance.
Medieval Wall in the Shopping Mall — One surviving portion of the old city wall has been cleverly incorporated into the modern Eyre Square Centre shopping mall. Medieval meets modern.
The Long Walk — The colorful row of houses along the waterfront is one of the most photographed streets in Ireland. The colors aren't just decorative — they helped fishermen identify their homes from the sea.
Pub music tip: Galway is one of the best cities in Ireland for live traditional music. Try Tigh Coili's for authentic trad sessions. The Claddagh Ring originated here — in the old Claddagh fishing village. This is the perfect place to buy one.

Ferry to Aran Islands, bike ride, Dún Aonghasa, beach time

Inishmore & the Aran Islands

Irish is the first language here — Inishmore is in the Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking region). Road signs are in Irish only. You'll hear Irish spoken casually — one of the few places left where it's a living daily language, not just a school subject.
Aran sweaters originated here — The distinctive cable-knit patterns were supposedly unique to each family, so drowned fishermen could be identified by their sweaters. Whether this is true or romantic legend is debated, but the sweaters are real and still hand-knitted on the islands.

Bike Inishmore

The 30-minute ride from the pier to Dún Aonghasa is one of Ireland's most popular cycling routes. Gentle roads, spectacular ocean views, ancient stone forts, and seal colonies. E-bikes available if you want an easier ride. Don't miss the Worm Hole — a natural rectangular pool where the sea ebbs and flows.

🔍 Dún Aonghasa
Originally 1,000 Meters From the Sea — When built, sea levels were much lower. Cliff erosion over millennia brought it to the edge. The "dramatic cliff fort" was originally well inland.
14 Acres of Defensive Space — Four concentric walls. Outside the third ring is a cheval de frise — upright limestone spikes designed to shred anyone charging the walls. Still well preserved.
1,270 Cubic Tons of Stone Without Mortar — Roughly the weight of 200 elephants, placed stone by stone, before mechanization.
Named After a Pre-Christian God — "Fort of Aonghas" — either a deity or mythical Iron Age king. Its exact construction date remains unknown despite intensive archaeology.
🔍 The Worm Hole (Poll na bPéist)
A Naturally Rectangular Pool — 25m long, 10m wide, carved along natural fault lines. The shape is so perfect it looks artificial. Connected to the Atlantic through underwater channels — the ocean surges in and drains rhythmically.
Red Bull Cliff Diving World Series (2014 & 2017) — World-class divers plunged from 27 meters (90 feet) into this pool. A natural formation became an international sports venue.
🔍 Other Inishmore
Megalithic Wedge Tomb — A burial chamber dating back 4,000 years (2000 BC). Older than the Egyptian pyramids. You're literally standing in a 4,000-year-old grave.
50+ Ancient Monuments — Beyond Dún Aonghasa, the island has over 50 ancient sites including Na Seacht dTeampaill (Seven Churches), St Benan's Church, and multiple stone forts. The whole island is an outdoor museum spanning 1,500+ years.
Kilmurvey Beach — Holds Blue Flag designation for water quality. Grey seals sunbathe on rocks at low tide (invisible at high tide). Contains rare plant species found nowhere else in Ireland. Also the main gateway to Dún Aonghasa — your hike starts here.
Dún Aonghasa is a prehistoric stone fort perched on top of 100-meter cliffs on Inishmore. It's over 3,000 years old, and there are no railings at the cliff edge — you can sit with your legs dangling over the Atlantic. Thrilling and terrifying in equal measure.
Souvenir alert: The Aran Islands are the birthplace of Aran sweaters. Traditional wool with intricate cable-knit patterns, each with a specific meaning. This is the most authentic place to buy one.

Cliffs of Moher, Limerick, travel to Killarney

Cliffs of Moher

The Cliffs of Moher were the "Cliffs of Insanity" in the movie The Princess Bride. They stretch 8km along the coast, reach 214 meters at their highest, and are home to puffins, razorbills, and peregrine falcons. On a clear day you can see the Aran Islands you just visited from O'Brien's Tower at the cliff edge.
The cliffs are 214m (702 ft) at their highest point — That's taller than a 60-story building. On a clear day, you can see the Aran Islands, Galway Bay, and the mountains of Connemara.
Harry Potter Connection — The Cliffs of Moher appear in Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince (2009) as the dramatic coastal cave where Dumbledore and Harry search for a Horcrux. The cave entrance scene was filmed here.
Ancient river channels are visible in the rock — Frozen into the 300-million-year-old stone, you can see the actual traces of rivers that flowed during the Carboniferous period. Time capsules of geological action.
The "white stripes" on sea stacks are bird droppings, not geology — What looks like geological layering is actually guano from massive guillemot colonies. 30,000 pairs of seabirds (20+ species) breed here at peak season — 60,000+ individual birds.
Guillemots can dive to 180 meters (600 feet) — These black-and-white seabirds launch from the 700-foot cliffs and can dive twice as deep as the cliff is tall. The cliffs are just their launching point.
O'Brien's Tower was built as a romantic gesture — Sir Cornelius O'Brien built it in 1835. Local stories say he built it to impress a woman he was courting. A major landmark built on love.
Hag's Head Viewpoint — Walk south from the main visitor center to the southernmost point of the cliffs. Far fewer tourists, better photography angles, different lighting. Most people never leave the main viewing area.
Woman's Head Rock Formation — At the southern end, a rock formation resembles a woman's profile gazing out to sea. Best viewed from the north. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.
Lost Town of Kilstiffen — Local folklore tells of an underwater town near the cliffs that sank when its chieftain lost a golden key. Ireland's own Atlantis legend.

Ring of Kerry, sheep farm visit

Ring of Kerry & Sheep Farm

The Ring of Kerry is 179km (111 miles) — Tour buses are required to drive counterclockwise. If you're in a car, go clockwise to avoid being stuck behind them.
Killarney National Park was Ireland's first national park — Established in 1932 when the Muckross Estate was donated to the state. It's 26,000 acres of mountains, lakes, and ancient oak woodland.
The Ring of Kerry is 112 miles of jaw-dropping coastal road around the Iveragh Peninsula. You'll see the vast Atlantic, the Lakes of Killarney, and the Macgillycuddy's Reeks (Ireland's tallest mountains). There are more sheep than people in County Kerry — you'll meet some up close at the farm visit.
Caherdaniel Fort (Hidden Chamber) — A lesser-known ring fort off the main Ring of Kerry route with a hidden chamber in the wall that most visitors miss entirely. Panoramic views of Derrynane Bay.
Torc Waterfall — A 60-foot cascading waterfall about 4 miles outside Killarney on the Kenmare road. Short walk from the parking area.
Ladies View — A famous viewpoint overlooking the Killarney Lakes, named because Queen Victoria's ladies-in-waiting were so enchanted by the view during the Queen's 1861 visit. Still one of the best panoramas in Ireland.
Charlie Chaplin Statue (Waterville) — A life-sized bronze statue of Charlie Chaplin in the tiny village of Waterville, where he vacationed with his family every summer from the 1950s to the 1970s. The village hosts an annual Charlie Chaplin Comedy Film Festival.
Moll's Gap — Named after Moll Kissane, a woman who ran a poitín (illegal moonshine) shop here in the early 19th century. Now a scenic mountain pass with a café and views of the MacGillycuddy's Reeks.
Fairy Forest Walk (Reenadinna Wood) — An ancient yew woodland on the Muckross Peninsula, one of the few remaining in Europe. The moss-covered trees and eerie atmosphere feel straight out of a fairy tale. Easy walk from Muckross House.
🔍 Sheep Farm Visit
The Galway Sheep Is Ireland's Only Native Wool-Producing Breed — Developed in the 17th century from English Longwool sheep. The wool is soft, dense, and long-stapled — ideal for Aran sweaters. But most "Irish sheep" today are actually imported breeds (Suffolks, Texels). The native breed is now a minority on Irish farms.
Ireland's Sheep Population Collapsed 63% in 20 Years — In 1985, Ireland had over 8 million sheep. By 2005, just 3 million. The international wool market collapsed due to synthetic garments like micro-fleece. A boom-to-bust within a single generation.
Aran Sweater Patterns Carry Coded Meaning — Cable stitch = fishermen's ropes and good fortune. Diamond stitch = wealth and success. Each pattern tells a story woven into the garment. Traditional clothing as a coded language.
Open Commonage Is Still Medieval — In upland areas, sheep are still managed in open commonage — a system virtually unchanged from medieval times where farmers graze on shared upland pastures. A living agricultural practice.

Blarney Castle & stone kiss, travel to Dublin

Blarney Castle

The Blarney Stone gets cleaned after each kiss using WHO-approved cleansers. You lean backwards over a gap to kiss it upside-down, 125+ steps up a spiral staircase. Legend says it gives you the "gift of the gab" for seven years.
"Blarney" entered the English language because of this castle — Cormac Láidir MacCarthy built the castle in 1446. When facing pressure from the English crown, he resisted through clever correspondence. Queen Elizabeth I called his evasive letters "nothing but Blarney" — giving the term its meaning: smooth talk used to deceive.
Over 400,000 people kiss the Blarney Stone each year — You have to lie on your back and lean backwards over a gap in the battlements to kiss it. The stone is cleaned regularly, despite the urban legends.
The Murder Hole — On the second floor (now blocked with an iron gate). Castle occupants used it to pour boiling water or oil on intruders climbing inside. Physics applied to warfare.
Cromwell's Forces Were Escaped Through Secret Tunnels — In 1646, when one of Cromwell's commanders arrived, the McCarthy clan made a hasty exit through secret underground tunnels beneath the castle.

Kiss the Blarney Stone

Climb 125+ narrow spiral steps, then lean backwards over a gap to kiss the stone upside-down. Staff help you and there are safety bars. Not ideal if you're afraid of heights, but absolutely worth it. Don't miss the gardens, Druid's Circle, and Witch's Cave.

The Poison Garden — Within the castle grounds, a garden of dangerous plants contained behind cage-like barriers. Educational and slightly creepy. The teens will love it.
The Wishing Steps — Steps carved through solid rock in the castle grounds leading to a waterfall. Legend says walking backwards down them with your eyes closed grants a wish.
Rock Close (2,000-Year-Old Yew Trees) — An ancient area within the grounds with yew trees over 2,000 years old and "druidic boulders" from a pre-castle settlement. Atmospheric and supposedly supernatural.
Badger's Cave — A historic cave through which Cromwell's forces supposedly fled during a siege. Hidden escape route from a real battle.
Hidden Rooms in the Tower — Explore the castle interior for hidden staircases, side chambers, and nested rooms — especially off the main kitchen area. It feels like solving a medieval puzzle.
Blarney pro tip: July means long lines. Budget 3–4 hours for the full castle and grounds experience. Wear comfortable shoes and bring water.

Irish Dance & Gaelic Games

Hurling is the fastest field sport in the world — A sliotar can exceed 160 km/h (100 mph) when struck by elite players. Faster than professional baseball pitches. Brought to Ireland by Celtic peoples approximately 3,000 years ago.
Irish dancers keep their arms rigid because... — The origin is debated. Popular theories: priests wanted dancing to look less suggestive, cramped dance spaces on tabletops or half-doors required stillness above the waist, or dancers wanted to emphasize the complexity of their footwork. Nobody knows for sure.
All GAA Players Are Amateur — Even at inter-county senior level. In an era of professional sports, elite athletes at the highest level play for glory only. No payment.
Hurling Scoring Is Unique — Goals (into the net) score 3 points; over-the-bar scores 1 point. "Dublin 3-5" means 3 goals (9 pts) + 5 points = 14 total. A hybrid system unique to this sport.
London Won the All-Ireland Hurling Championship — In 1901. The only non-Irish team ever to do so. A historical anomaly that never repeated.
The GAA Is a Cultural Preservation Body, Not Just a Sports Organization — Its official objectives include promoting the Irish language, music, song, and dance. A sports org functioning as an official culture keeper.

Irish Dance Class

You'll learn traditional ceili steps and maybe some Riverdance-style moves. It's high-energy, everyone looks a bit silly at first, and it's an absolute blast as a group activity.

Gaelic Games

You'll actually participate in Ireland's native sports: hurling (a stick-and-ball game that's insanely fast) and Gaelic football (a mix of soccer and rugby). These sports are over 2,000 years old and deeply woven into Irish identity.

Jul 22 — Dublin
James Joyce Museum, EPIC Emigration Museum, farewell dinner

Dublin — Final Day

Dublin is a UNESCO City of Literature — home to James Joyce, Oscar Wilde, Bram Stoker (creator of Dracula), Samuel Beckett, and W.B. Yeats. You'll visit the James Joyce Museum to learn about the author of Ulysses, one of the most influential novels ever written.
The EPIC Emigration Museum is located at the original departure point for Irish emigrants. Over 10 million people left Ireland between 1800 and 1950 — more than the current population. The interactive museum explores why they left and how they shaped the world.
🔍 James Joyce Tower (Sandycove)
The Revolver Incident — Joyce lived in this Martello tower for only 6 nights in September 1904. On the sixth night, housemate Trench fired his pistol at an imaginary panther while Joyce slept. Gogarty (fictionalized as "Buck Mulligan") then deliberately shot the pots above Joyce's head. Joyce stormed out so angrily he walked 8 miles back to Dublin without even collecting his belongings.
The Tower Opens Ulysses — The very first scene takes place on the roof. You can stand in the exact spot where Buck Mulligan held up his shaving bowl "introit-wise."
The Tower Was Built to Defend Against Napoleon — One of a network of Martello towers built by the British in the early 1800s against a Napoleonic invasion that never occurred. Military architecture famous for having nothing to do with its purpose.
Sylvia Beach Opened It as a Museum — On June 16, 1962 (Bloomsday), the publisher of Ulysses herself opened the museum. Contains the first edition of Ulysses (1922), Joyce's letters, photos, and his death mask.
🔍 EPIC The Irish Emigration Museum
70 Million People Globally Claim Irish Heritage — Over 10 million emigrated; 70 million now claim Irish heritage worldwide. Ireland's population is 5 million. A 14:1 ratio showing the scale of historical emigration.
23 U.S. Presidents Have Irish Ancestry — From James Buchanan to Joe Biden. The Irish diaspora literally shaped American political leadership.
The Oscar Statuette Was Designed by an Irish-American — Cedric Gibbons, featured in the museum's exhibits. Every Academy Award carries the fingerprints of Irish-descended design.
The Passport Stamps — You receive a "passport" at entry that you stamp at each exhibit. By the end, your passport is full of stamps telling the story of Irish emigration. A clever souvenir built into the experience.
20 Individually Themed Galleries — Organized under four themes: Migration (patterns since 500 AD), Motivation (reasons), Influence (diaspora achievements), and Diaspora Today. Covers religious missionaries, the Famine, criminal transportation to Australia, and more — not just a "fleeing poverty" narrative.
🔍 Temple Bar
Rory Gallagher's Guitar — An exact replica of rock legend Rory Gallagher's famous Fender Stratocaster displayed high on a building at "Rory's Corner" in Temple Bar. You have to look UP to spot it.
Temple Bar Isn't Named After a Pub — The area is named after Sir William Temple, who owned land here in the 17th century. "Bar" refers to a riverside walkway. The pubs came later.
It Was Dublin's Red Light District in the 1700s — After the customs house arrived in 1707, warehouses, taverns, theatres, and brothels flourished. Before becoming Europe's most famous party district, it was famous for an entirely different reason.
It Nearly Became a Giant Bus Depot — After 200 years of urban decay, the government planned to build a bus depot here. Protests prevented it. Today's cultural district exists because people said "no" to buses.
Handel's Messiah Premiered Next Door — On April 13, 1742, Handel's Messiah premiered on Fishamble Street, a stone's throw from Temple Bar. An annual performance is still held at the same location on the same date.
The United Irishmen Were Founded in a Tavern on Eustace Street — The pivotal republican revolutionary group was founded at a meeting in a Temple Bar tavern in 1791.
