Etiquette & Social Customs

Americans are generally well-liked in Scotland and Ireland, but a few cultural differences can trip you up. Here's how to blend in and show respect:

The Big Ones (Don't Get These Wrong)

Never call a Scot "English" or confuse Scotland with England. Scotland is its own country with its own culture, legal system, and identity. This is genuinely offensive, not just a mild annoyance.
Never call an Irish person "British." The Republic of Ireland is an independent nation. Only Northern Ireland is part of the UK. This is deeply important to Irish people and getting it wrong is one of the fastest ways to cause offense.
Skip the stereotypes. No "top o' the morning" (nobody says this), no leprechaun jokes, and definitely no potato famine jokes. These range from eye-roll-worthy to deeply hurtful.

Conversation & Humor

Scottish Humor

Scots love dry wit, sarcasm, and self-deprecation. If someone "slags you off" (teases you), it's a sign of affection, not hostility. The more they mock you, the more they like you. Join in rather than getting defensive.

Irish Humor

The Irish are world-class storytellers with razor-sharp wit. "Slagging" (teasing) is central to Irish friendships. If someone gently roasts you, they're welcoming you. Laugh along and try to give it back.

Safe Topics

Local recommendations, music, sports (ask about their team rather than assume), travel, weather, and genuine questions about the culture. People in both countries love when visitors show real curiosity.

Topics to Tread Carefully Around

Scottish independence, Brexit, Northern Ireland politics and "The Troubles," and reunification debates. These are deeply personal issues with strong feelings on all sides. Don't bring them up — but if a local does, listen more than you talk.

Pub Etiquette Both

Order at the Bar

In most pubs, there's no table service. Walk to the bar, find a spot, and wait your turn. Don't shout, wave, snap, or whistle — the bartender sees you and will get to you. Patience is key.

Buy Your Round

If you're drinking with a group, buying rounds is sacred. One person buys for everyone, then the next person does. Not buying your round is a serious social violation. If someone buys you a drink, plan to return the favor.

Don't Tip Per Drink

Unlike the US, you don't tip the bartender for each drink. At the end of a long night, you can say "and one for yourself" to offer the bartender a drink, but it's not expected.

Dining Etiquette Both

They won't bring the bill. In both countries, the waiter will never bring your check unless you ask. They consider it rude to rush you out. When you're ready, catch their eye and say "Can I get the bill?" or you'll be sitting there all night.

Splitting the bill is perfectly fine — just mention it upfront. A gentle "argument" over who pays is part of Irish culture and entirely good-natured. If a service charge is already on the bill, no additional tip is needed.

Queuing (Lines) Both

Queuing is practically a religion. Never cut in line, never crowd the person in front of you, and never ask someone to hold your spot. Americans tend to be more casual about lines — resist that instinct here. Cutting a queue will earn immediate and vocal disapproval.

Volume & Manners Both

Americans are noticeably louder than locals in both countries. You don't need to whisper, but dial it down a notch, especially in pubs, restaurants, on public transport, and in churches. Say "please" and "thank you" generously — locals absolutely notice this and it goes a long way. Hold doors for the person behind you.

In Churches & Cathedrals

You'll visit St. Giles Cathedral, St. Patrick's Cathedral, and others. Cover shoulders and knees, keep your voice low, silence your phone, and don't photograph during services. These are active places of worship, not just tourist attractions. Ask before using your camera inside.

Photography

Outdoor photography is generally fine everywhere. In museums and historic sites, respect "no photography" and "no flash" signs. Flash damages paintings, fades textiles, and weakens ancient documents like the Book of Kells. Ask permission before photographing people up close, especially musicians in pubs.
